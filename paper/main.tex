\title{A Diffusion World Model Times a Hidden Event Only Where a Context Frame Sits:\\
A Pre-registered Study on Ms.~Pac-Man}
\author{Sudish Mulakala}
\date{Draft, \today}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Pixel diffusion world models predict the next frame from a short context of past frames, so they cannot see state the
screen does not show. We study one such state, a \emph{hidden timer}, in an action-conditioned diffusion model of
Ms.~Pac-Man in the style of DIAMOND~\cite{alonso2024diamond}. The game holds ghosts in a pen for up to 91 steps with
no on-screen countdown. Models that see only the last 4 or 8 frames park the ghosts: in 450-step rollouts on held-out
episodes, 16--23 of 30 rollouts keep the pen occupied for 200+ steps, against none in the real game. A context of 10
frames with a 97-step \emph{reach} (4 recent frames plus 6 frames 16 steps apart) removes the parking (0/30; paired
difference to 4 frames $-0.57$, 95\% CI $[-0.77,-0.37]$ over episodes).
We state a rule: a model can time a hidden event of period $N$ only as precisely as its context offsets resolve $-N$.
In a pre-registered sweep of 23 small models on a synthetic game, scored against an ideal observer limited to the same
offsets, the rule holds \emph{partially}: no model times a period beyond its reach, and models with a frame exactly
at $-N$ match the observer at three of four long periods, but a period between two frames parks. A pre-registered
follow-up changes one offset: moving a frame from $-81$ to $-80$ takes the $N{=}80$ model from parking in 37 of 60
starts to parking in none, while three periods between frames of the new layout park more than the observer allows
(one of them by a single start).
On a second real game (the 2600 Pac-Man) the registered test failed: the strided model releases the ghost in 0.99
of starts but with a median timing error of 12 steps against a bar of 5. The hidden period there falls between two
context frames, which is consistent with the synthetic finding but was not a registered prediction.
We also report failed predictions and negative results in full: DIAMOND's released model loses its ghosts instead of
parking them; two latent-action models trained without labels fail, the first by ignoring its codes and the second by
spending them on ghosts; and several earlier claims do not survive episode-level confidence intervals.
Every experiment was pre-registered before its results existed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Diffusion world models generate convincing game frames from a short context of past frames and actions
\cite{alonso2024diamond,valevski2024gamengen}. Their short context makes them vulnerable to state that the screen does
not show. Many games run clocks with no on-screen countdown: in Ms.~Pac-Man, ghosts wait in their pen for a fixed time
after a death, and a power pellet's effect lasts 124--134 steps. A model that sees only its last few frames cannot know
how long a ghost has been penned.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A documented failure and a fix.} With a 4- or 8-frame context, our 18.8M-parameter diffusion model parks
ghosts in the pen; a strided context with a 97-step reach removes the parking (Sec.~\ref{sec:context}).
\item \textbf{A testable rule with an ideal-observer prediction,} and a pre-registered synthetic sweep in which it
holds partially (Sec.~\ref{sec:rule}).
\item \textbf{A one-variable follow-up} showing that timing depends on where the context frames sit, not only on how
far they reach: moving one frame by one step fixes a parked period (Sec.~\ref{sec:followup}).
\item \textbf{A pre-registered evaluation that reports its failures:} a second real game that fails on timing
(Sec.~\ref{sec:realgame}), a failed prediction about DIAMOND (Sec.~\ref{sec:diamond}) and two failed latent-action
models (Sec.~\ref{sec:lam}). CIs are bootstrapped over held-out episodes.
\end{enumerate}
Many paired comparisons are reported without correction for multiple comparisons; intervals that barely exclude zero
should be read with caution.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Data.} \texttt{ALE/MsPacman-v5}~\cite{bellemare2013ale}, 4-frame skip with max-pooling over all 4 frames,
because Ms.~Pac-Man draws one ghost per frame and 2-frame pooling (the DQN default~\cite{mnih2015dqn}) shows all four
ghosts in only 29--47\% of observations. Maze crop $172\times160$, area-resized to $64\times64$. A PPO agent with
$\varepsilon$-random and sticky-random exploration recorded 2.2M frames (3,879 episodes). The validation split is frozen
by checksum: 388 episodes.

\paragraph{Model.} An EDM~\cite{karras2022edm} denoising UNet (18.8M params; widths 64/96/192/192, attention at 16 and
8 px). The noisy next frame is concatenated with $K$ context frames, and the actions enter through adaptive group
normalisation. It is trained with GameNGen-style context-noise augmentation~\cite{valevski2024gamengen} and sampled
with 3 Euler steps. Training: 100k steps at learning rate $10^{-4}$, followed by a 15k-step anneal at $10^{-5}$.

\paragraph{Context layouts.} The single variable of the ablation is the set of context offsets: \textbf{ctx4}
($-4..-1$), \textbf{ctx8} ($-8..-1$), and \textbf{ctx6s16} ($-97,-81,\dots,-17,-4..-1$: 10 frames, reach 97).

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Rollouts of 450 steps from step 100 of the 10 longest held-out episodes, 3 sampler seeds each,
replaying the recorded actions. Pixel detectors, validated on real frames, measure:
\begin{itemize}
\item Pac-Man position error;
\item wall IoU and pellet IoU;
\item ghost count, gated by RAM;
\item responsiveness: whether the model's Pac-Man turns where the recorded action turns him;
\item pen occupancy: any non-background pixels in the pen box.
\end{itemize}
CIs are 95\% bootstrap intervals over the 10 episodes (10,000 resamples). Each resampled episode keeps all three of its
seeds.

\paragraph{What holds.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Strided context removes the parking: the parked fraction falls by $-0.567$ $[-0.767,-0.367]$ against ctx4, and
by $-0.833$ $[-0.933,-0.733]$ in 900-step rollouts.
\item It also lowers Pac-Man error at 450 steps: $-5.3$ px $[-11.6,-0.5]$.
\item The LR anneal improves responsiveness: $+0.044$ $[+0.009,+0.080]$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What does not hold.}
\begin{itemize}
\item There is no clear evidence that eight frames park more than four: $+0.20$ $[-0.10,+0.47]$.
\item There is no clear evidence that ten times more data changes parking ($+0.03$ $[-0.20,+0.27]$) or Pac-Man
error ($-0.5$ px $[-2.7,+1.3]$); only responsiveness improves ($+0.071$ $[+0.014,+0.129]$).
\item No difference in ghost count between our models is significant.
\item A $128\times128$ version is sharper per step but drifts faster: Pac-Man error $+8.2$ px $[+1.9,+13.9]$ at
64px-equivalent.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The second timer is not solved.} The frightened timer (124--134 steps) exceeds the 97-step reach.
\begin{itemize}
\item No model ends frightened phases on time.
\item A 145-step-reach model (13 frames) ended them too early, and weakened the pen timer that 10 frames had fixed.
\item Teacher-forced analysis shows every model copies the phase end one step after it appears in a real frame, instead
of predicting it. The phase end is a one-frame event about once per episode, which the denoising loss barely rewards.
\end{itemize}
Reach therefore appears \emph{necessary but not sufficient}.

\section{The hidden-timer rule}
\label{sec:rule}
\paragraph{Rule.} An autoregressive frame model sees only its context. It can time a hidden-timer event of period $N$
only as precisely as its context offsets resolve $-N$:
\begin{itemize}
\item exactly, if $-N$ is an offset;
\item spread over the gap, if $-N$ falls between two offsets;
\item not at all beyond the reach $R$. The best possible behaviour is then a geometric wait with hazard $1/(N-R)$, and a
sampling model may do worse by parking.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Ideal observer.} For each layout we estimate, from training data, the hazard
$P(\text{event next}\mid\text{state at the layout's offsets})$, and roll this observer out from the same test starts
as the models. It is the best that \emph{any} model with those offsets can do, and its curve is the rule's prediction.
It is computed and committed before any model trains.

\paragraph{Synthetic game.}
\begin{itemize}
\item $16\times16$ frames. A ghost is penned for exactly $N$ frames; nothing on screen changes with elapsed time.
\item 23 cells cross three layouts with $N\in[3,200]$: C4 and C10 are consecutive with reach 4 and 10; S10 is ctx6s16's
layout, reach 97.
\item Small versions of our model, 15k steps each.
\item Pre-registered criteria: in reach, the model must come close to the observer; beyond reach, it must not beat an
observer blind to the capture.
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Criteria.} R1 (exact offset in reach): on-time fraction (tolerance 2) $\ge\min(0.80,\text{ideal}-0.10)$.
R2 (gap in reach): release fraction $\ge\min(0.90,\text{ideal}-0.10)$ and median $|\text{lag}-N|\le$ ideal median $+3$.
R3 (out of reach, $N\ge1.5R$): on-time fraction (tolerance $\max(2,0.1N)$) $\le$ ideal $+0.15$. R4: at
$N\in\{33,65,97\}$, S10 passes R1 and C10 passes R3. The rule \emph{holds} if all pass, holds \emph{partially} if every
R3 cell passes but some R1/R2 cell fails, and \emph{fails} if any R3 cell fails. One R2 bar was amended before any model
trained, because the observer itself parks in the gap cells; the amendment is in the pre-registration.

\paragraph{Verdict: the rule holds partially.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No model times a period beyond its reach.} All 10 R3 cells pass. Instead of the observer's geometric
wait, the out-of-reach models mostly \emph{park}: they release in 0.12--0.30 of starts at long periods (C4
$N\ge33$; C10 and S10 $N\ge65$), against 0.89--0.98 for the observer. This reproduces the Ms.~Pac-Man pen parking in a
game with no other cause. R3 is one-sided, so it shows that no model beat a blind observer; it does not show that the
models behave like one.
\item \textbf{An exact offset gives exact timing when the observer is reliable.} All R1 cells but one pass, and at
S10 $N{=}17,33,65$ the model is within 0.04 of the observer. At S10 $N{=}65$ the released lags have quartiles
65/65/65.
\item \textbf{Two in-reach cells fail.} At S10 $N{=}97$ the model is on time in 0.167 of starts against a bar of
0.199. The CI contains the bar, but point estimates decide. This is the weakest test in the sweep, flagged before
training: the $-97$ frame often falls inside the previous stay, so even the observer is on time in only 0.299. At S10
$N{=}80$, a gap cell, the model releases near $N$ when it releases (lag quartiles 73/76/78.5), but parks in 37 of 60
starts. Its release fraction is 0.383 $[0.267, 0.500]$ against a bar of 0.494.
\item R4 holds at $N{=}33$ and $65$ and fails at $97$.
\end{itemize}
Reach is therefore necessary but not sufficient at this training budget. A gap, or a far offset that is often masked,
leads to parking or spread timing, which is a cost of striding.

\subsection{Follow-up: moving one frame}
\label{sec:followup}
The S10 layout has frames at $-81$ and $-65$, so $N{=}80$ falls between two frames, and that cell parked. We
pre-registered one follow-up before running it. The single change is the layout \textbf{S10b}: S10 with the frame at
$-81$ moved to $-80$ (same reach, same frame count; same game, model, steps, seed, starts and scoring). The registered
prediction: the on-frame period ($N{=}80$) passes R1, and periods between frames of S10b ($N{=}72$, 79, 88) \emph{park},
defined as a release fraction below the R2 release bar. The ideal observer, computed first, disagreed in part: it
releases in about 0.9 of starts mid-gap ($N{=}72$, 88) and in 0.59 at the end of a gap ($N{=}79$).

\paragraph{Verdict: supported, as registered.}
\begin{itemize}
\item With the frame at $-81$, the $N{=}80$ model parked in 37 of 60 starts. With it at $-80$, it parks in none, and
its released lags have quartiles 80/80/81.
\item All three off-frame cells park. $N{=}79$ and $N{=}88$ fail clearly; $N{=}72$ fails by one start (47 of 60
released, 48 needed), and its CI contains the bar.
\item The observer predicted the ordering (79 worst), but the models park more than it does in every gap cell, by
0.11 to 0.32.
\item \textbf{Caveat.} In the main sweep the two short gap cells (S10 at $N{=}8$ and $N{=}24$) released in 1.00 and
0.82 of starts and passed R2. ``Between frames parks'' therefore holds for the far frames here, not for every gap.
This comparison across the two experiments is an observation, not a registered test.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{A second real game}
\label{sec:realgame}
\paragraph{Design.} The 2600 Pac-Man holds a ghost in its house for exactly 35 or 36 steps after every death. The
interval starts at a visible event, has no on-screen countdown, and is detectable at 64px in 622 of 622 held-out
stays. We recorded 1M steps of random play and trained the Ms.~Pac-Man recipe. The observer predicts a large contrast:
S10 on time in 0.889 of starts, C10 in 0.101. The registered criteria: pac-S10 must release in at least 0.898 of
starts with a median timing error of at most 5 steps (P-S), and pac-C10 must not beat a blind observer (P-C).

\paragraph{Attempt 1 stopped at its pilot.} A registered pilot required pac-S10, after 20k of 115k steps, to release
in at least 0.50 of starts. It released in 0.486 $[0.445, 0.525]$.

\paragraph{Attempt 2 failed on timing.} We registered the full run separately, with the same bars and with P-S as the
gate for training pac-C10. After 115k steps pac-S10 releases in 0.986 $[0.976, 0.994]$ of starts and parks in 9 of 622,
but its median timing error is 12 $[10, 13]$ steps against a bar of 5, and it is on time in 0.22 $[0.18, 0.25]$ of
starts against the observer's 0.89. Its lags have 10th/25th/50th/75th/90th percentiles 28/33/44/72/96 against a truth
of 35--36. P-S fails, pac-C10 was not trained, and the rule is not confirmed on this game. Before the 15k-step
low-LR anneal the same model released in only 0.678 of starts with a median error of 65.

\paragraph{A post-hoc reading.} The 35--36-step stay falls between S10's frames at $-33$ and $-49$. Loose timing and
parking between frames is what the synthetic follow-up found, so the failure is consistent with it. This reading was
made after the result and is not a registered test; no model with a frame at $-35$ or $-36$ was trained.

\section{External reference point: DIAMOND}
\label{sec:diamond}
\paragraph{What we ran.} DIAMOND released its trained Atari-100k Ms.~Pac-Man world model (4-frame context). We ran it
without retraining: we re-simulated our held-out episodes in its preprocessing, which includes the score area and
2-frame pooling, and rebuilt our detectors for its geometry. All validity checks passed.

\paragraph{The registered prediction failed.} We had predicted that it would park ghosts in at least 10 of 30
rollouts. It parked in 0/30. We report this as a failed prediction and do not redefine the test.

\paragraph{Why it does not test the rule.} Parking can only be measured while ghosts exist, and DIAMOND's ghosts
vanish (Fig.~\ref{fig:ghosts}): its ghost count at 450 steps is 0.077 $[0.054,0.097]$ of its own ground truth, against
0.41 $[0.25,0.61]$ for our 4-frame Model~1 and 0.58 $[0.30,0.87]$ for our served model. The vanishing is not a detector
artifact: over the first 15 steps the detectors find 0.99 $[0.97,1.01]$ of the ground-truth ghost count in DIAMOND's
generated frames, and 3.86 ghosts per ground-truth frame where 4 should be visible. The result is neither support for
the rule nor evidence against it.

\paragraph{Other metrics.} DIAMOND keeps the walls best (wall IoU / own ceiling 1.000 $[0.995,1.004]$) and is worse on
Pac-Man error (25.3 px $[19.1,31.2]$ vs 14.6 $[10.4,19.3]$ for our served model) and responsiveness (0.18
$[0.13,0.24]$ vs 0.43 $[0.40,0.46]$).

\paragraph{A reference point, not a head-to-head.} The two models differ in purpose (15-step imagination for RL vs
long interactive rollouts), training data (100k steps of DIAMOND's own agent, with episodes ending at each life loss,
vs 2.2M frames of a PPO agent with exploration: about $1/20$ of our data), size (4.4M vs 18.8M parameters) and frame
format (full screen with 2-frame pooling vs maze crop with 4-frame pooling). A 450-step rollout is 30 times DIAMOND's
design horizon. The comparison measures long-horizon coherence, not the merit of either design.

\section{Learned controls: two negative results}
\label{sec:lam}
\paragraph{Setup.} We trained a Genie-style latent action model~\cite{bruce2024genie} on frames only: 8 codes, a VQ
bottleneck~\cite{vandenoord2017vqvae} and a decoder built on the world model's UNet, under a label firewall tested by
double runs with scrambled labels. The loss is weighted around Pac-Man with a pixel detector (v1 also had an
unweighted arm).

\paragraph{v1: the decoder ignored the code.} With the 97-step context, both arms collapsed to one or two codes and
code gain (shuffled-code error over inferred-code error) was 1.000. The pre-registered stop fired before any label was
read.

\paragraph{v2: the collapse was fixed, but the codes captured the ghosts.} v2 changed five things, registered in
advance: a decoder that sees only the last frame, an entropy term on code usage~\cite{yu2024magvit2} with its weight
tied to the loss scale, non-zero initial code influence, a single player-weighted arm, and the code as extra input
channels. A 2,500-step pilot had three conditions. Perplexity was 7.42 ($\ge 4$: pass) and code gain 1.020
($>1.01$: pass). The third asked where the codes act: over 1,000 held-out contexts decoded with all 8 codes, the
share of the output variance near Pac-Man had to exceed the share near ghosts. It was 0.067 near Pac-Man against 0.400
near ghosts (per pixel, twice as large near ghosts). The pilot failed and the run stopped.

\paragraph{Mechanism.} The data come from a single recording agent whose moves are largely predictable from the
screen, so a code has little to add about the player. With a short decoder context the ghosts are the largest
surprise in the next frame, and there are up to four of them, so the codes describe ghosts. No controllability result
exists for either version, and we have closed this line of work.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item One game for the main study, 64px frames, one seed per training configuration, and 10 evaluation episodes,
hence the wide CIs.
\item The synthetic tests isolate timing but have no action conditioning, and uses one seed per cell. Its
out-of-reach criterion is one-sided, so parking passes it.
\item The second real game failed its registered test, and its C10 model was never trained, so the rule has no
confirmation on a second real game. The strided run there was resumed once from the pilot's 20k-step checkpoint: the
learning rate (constant after warm-up), weights, EMA and optimizer state match an uninterrupted run, but the batch and
noise stream was re-seeded, so it is not bit-identical to one.
\item The follow-up is one layout, one seed per cell and 60 starts per cell; one of its three off-frame cells is
marginal.
\item Many paired comparisons are reported without multiple-comparison correction.
\item The frightened timer remains unsolved.
\item Responsiveness on replayed trajectories partly measures how predictable the agent is, not controllability.
\item The pen-occupancy measure is not resolution-invariant; at 128px its own ground truth differs.
\end{itemize}
